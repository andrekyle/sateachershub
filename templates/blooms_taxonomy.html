1. What is Bloom's Taxonomy?

Bloom's Taxonomy is a classification of the different levels of thinking that learners use when they engage with content. It was first published in 1956 by a committee of educators chaired by American educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom, and it has since become the world's most widely used framework for designing learning outcomes, lesson activities, questions, and assessments.

In South Africa, the Curriculum and Assessment Policy Statement (CAPS) uses an adapted Bloom's framework to specify the cognitive demand of every assessment task — from a Grade 4 Natural Sciences worksheet to a Grade 12 NSC examination. Setting a balanced paper means hitting the right percentage of marks at each cognitive level.

Why it matters for SA teachers: The DBE moderates papers against Bloom's levels. A Grade 12 Mathematics paper that has 80% recall and 20% problem-solving will be rejected, even if every question is mathematically correct. Cognitive balance is non-negotiable.

2. Original (1956) vs. Revised (2001)

In 2001, Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl (one of Bloom's original collaborators) published a revised version. The two key changes:

  1. Nouns became verbs. "Knowledge" → "Remember", "Comprehension" → "Understand", and so on. This emphasises that learning is something a learner does, not has.
  2. The top two levels swapped. "Synthesis" became "Create" and was placed at the top, above "Evaluate".
Original (1956)Revised (2001)Short description
KnowledgeRememberRecall facts and basic concepts
ComprehensionUnderstandExplain ideas or concepts
ApplicationApplyUse information in new situations
AnalysisAnalyseDraw connections among ideas
SynthesisEvaluateJustify a stand or decision
EvaluationCreateProduce new or original work

This guide uses the revised 2001 version, which is the version aligned with current CAPS and DBE assessment frameworks.

Currency note: The dates 1956 and 2001 are historical publication dates for the taxonomy itself. The practical guidance in this page is checked against 2024-2026 DBE sources (CAPS updates, NSC reports, and current policy pages).

3. The Six Cognitive Levels (at a glance)

The taxonomy is hierarchical: each level builds on the ones below. A learner cannot truly analyse a poem if they cannot first remember its content and understand what it says.

CreateHigher-order thinking
Evaluate
Analyse
Apply
Understand
RememberLower-order thinking

Lower-order thinking skills (LOTS): Remember, Understand, Apply.
Higher-order thinking skills (HOTS): Analyse, Evaluate, Create.

Recent NSC reporting (2024 onward) continues to emphasize balanced cognitive demand. The exact LOTS/HOTS split is subject-specific and should always be checked against the latest DBE subject guidance and reports.

4. The Six Levels in Detail

Level 1

Remember

Lower-order · Recall

Retrieve relevant knowledge from long-term memory. The learner reproduces a fact, term, definition, formula or procedure exactly as it was taught.

Action verbs

DefineListNameStateIdentifyRecallLabelMatchRecogniseReciteSelectQuote

Question stems

"List the three branches of the South African government."

"Define the term photosynthesis."

"Name two characters in the prescribed novel."

Level 2

Understand

Lower-order · Comprehension

Construct meaning from instructional messages — explain in own words, summarise, classify, infer, compare and exemplify.

Action verbs

ExplainDescribeSummariseParaphraseClassifyCompareConvertIllustrateInterpretDiscussOutlineTranslate

Question stems

"Explain in your own words how a thunderstorm forms."

"Summarise the main argument of paragraph 3."

"Describe the difference between weather and climate."

Level 3

Apply

Lower-order · Routine procedures

Carry out or use a procedure in a given situation. The context may be new, but the method itself was taught.

Action verbs

CalculateSolveDemonstrateUseApplyComputeConstructModifyOperatePredictShowImplement

Question stems

"Calculate the gradient of the line passing through (2,3) and (5,11)."

"Apply the law of conservation of momentum to this collision."

"Use the COUNTIF function to count the cells greater than 50."

Level 4

Analyse

Higher-order · Break down

Break material into parts and detect how the parts relate to one another and to an overall structure or purpose. Differentiate, organise, attribute.

Action verbs

AnalyseCompareContrastDifferentiateDistinguishExamineCategoriseInvestigateDeduceInferTestRelate

Question stems

"Analyse the poet's use of imagery in the second stanza."

"Distinguish between fixed and variable costs in this scenario."

"Examine how soil type affects crop yield in the data shown."

Level 5

Evaluate

Higher-order · Judge

Make judgements based on criteria and standards. Critique, justify, defend, recommend — backed by evidence.

Action verbs

EvaluateAssessJustifyCritiqueDefendRecommendArgueJudgeRankValidateSupportPrioritise

Question stems

"Evaluate the effectiveness of the government's load-shedding policy."

"Justify your choice of marketing strategy with reference to the case study."

"Critically discuss whether the experiment was a fair test."

Level 6

Create

Higher-order · Produce

Put elements together to form a coherent or functional whole; reorganise elements into a new pattern, structure or product.

Action verbs

CreateDesignComposeConstructDevelopFormulatePlanProduceGenerateInventProposeHypothesise

Question stems

"Design a database to track learners' attendance for a school of 800 pupils."

"Write a 250-word speech persuading Grade 9s to choose Mathematics."

"Propose a research question and outline a method to investigate it."

5. The Knowledge Dimension

The 2001 revision added a second axis: what kind of knowledge the learner is working with. A good question paper covers all four types, not just facts.

TypeWhat it isExample
FactualDiscrete pieces of information — terminology, specific details"In which year was the Union of South Africa formed?"
ConceptualRelationships among basic elements — categories, principles, models"Explain how supply and demand interact to set a market price."
ProceduralHow to do something — methods, techniques, algorithms"Solve for x using the quadratic formula."
MetacognitiveKnowledge about cognition — strategies, self-awareness"Reflect on which study method worked best for you and explain why."

6. How Bloom's Maps to CAPS Cognitive Levels

CAPS does not use the six Bloom's labels directly. Instead, the DBE collapses them into four cognitive levels, each with a recommended weighting per question paper. The exact weighting differs by subject, but the framework below is standard.

CAPS levelBloom's equivalentTypical % of marksDescription
Level 1 — KnowledgeRemember20–30%Direct recall of facts, terminology, formulae
Level 2 — Routine proceduresUnderstand + Apply30–40%Familiar examples, single-step procedures
Level 3 — Complex proceduresAnalyse20–30%Multi-step problems, unfamiliar contexts
Level 4 — Problem-solvingEvaluate + Create10–20%Non-routine, justification, design, original response

7. A Worked Question Paper Blueprint

Suppose you are setting a 100-mark Grade 12 Geography paper. A CAPS-compliant Bloom's distribution looks like this:

LevelMarksQuestion type examples
Remember (L1)25MCQs, term definitions, labelling a diagram
Understand + Apply (L2)35Explain a process, interpret a graph, calculate gradient on a topographic map
Analyse (L3)25Compare two climate regions, deduce cause from data
Evaluate + Create (L4)15Recommend a sustainable land-use plan with justification

When you finish setting your paper, total the marks for each level and check the percentages — that's your cognitive analysis grid, which moderators will want to see.

8. Subject-by-Subject Application

Below are CAPS-aligned cognitive blueprints and worked examples for the major South African subjects. Use these as starting points; each School Based Assessment (SBA) and final exam may adjust slightly.

HOTS means Higher-Order Thinking Skills: questions that require learners to analyse, evaluate, or create, rather than only recall facts.

Mathematics (Grade 12)

L1:20% — Recall definitions, identify formulas (e.g. "State the cosine rule") L2:35% — Routine calculations (factorise, solve linear equation, basic differentiation) L3:30% — Multi-step problems (optimise volume, prove a trig identity, related rates) L4:15% — Unfamiliar contexts, proofs, modelling real-world data

Example HOTS question: "A farmer has 200 m of fencing to enclose a rectangular grazing area against a river (no fencing required on the river side). Determine the dimensions that maximise the enclosed area, and justify why your answer is a maximum."

Mathematical Literacy

L1:30% — Read directly from a table, identify a unit of measurement L2:30% — Apply a known formula to a familiar context (calculate VAT, perimeter) L3:20% — Interpret data sets, compare two tariffs L4:20% — Reason and reflect on real-life decisions (best cell-phone contract, financial planning)

Example HOTS question: "Compare the two electricity tariff structures (prepaid vs. post-paid) for a household using 450 kWh per month. Recommend the better option and justify with calculations."

Physical Sciences

L1:15% — Recall definitions, laws (Newton's three laws, Ohm's law) L2:35% — Substitution into formulae, balance equations, name compounds L3:35% — Analyse circuits, multi-step stoichiometry, projectile motion L4:15% — Design an experiment, evaluate data validity, predict outcomes

Example HOTS question: "A learner claims that the rate of reaction depends only on temperature. Design an experiment to test this claim, identify the dependent and independent variables, and explain how you would control bias."

Life Sciences

L1:40% — Knowledge of structures, terms, processes (mitosis stages) L2:25% — Comprehension and application (predict offspring genotype) L3:20% — Analyse, evaluate (interpret graphs, ecosystems data) L4:15% — Synthesis (propose conservation strategy)

Example HOTS question: "A population of impala in a reserve has dropped by 35% over five years. Analyse the data table provided, identify the most likely cause, and propose two intervention strategies. Justify each."

Accounting

L1:10% — Recall accounting equation, ledger conventions L2:40% — Routine recording (journals, posting, balancing) L3:30% — Prepare financial statements with adjustments L4:20% — Interpret ratios, advise management, ethics scenarios

Example HOTS question: "The current ratio dropped from 2.1 to 1.2 in one year. Identify three possible causes, evaluate the implications for the business, and recommend remedial action."

Business Studies

L1:30% — Define terms, list factors of production L2:30% — Explain concepts, describe types of businesses L3:25% — Apply theory to case studies, analyse business challenges L4:15% — Evaluate strategies, recommend with justification

Example HOTS question: "Read the case study on XYZ Manufacturing. Evaluate management's response to the strike action, and recommend an alternative approach using two strategies from the prescribed content."

Economics

L1:30% — Define elasticity, GDP, inflation L2:30% — Explain market mechanisms, draw a demand curve L3:25% — Analyse trade data, examine policy effects L4:15% — Evaluate the SARB's interest rate decision and motivate a counter-proposal

Geography

L1:25% — Identify features, define terms (e.g. "What is a cold front?") L2:30% — Map work calculations, climate explanations L3:30% — Analyse synoptic charts, compare urban patterns L4:15% — Suggest sustainable solutions, evaluate land-use conflict

History

L1:20% — Recall dates, names, events (Sharpeville, 1960) L2:30% — Explain causes and consequences in own words L3:30% — Source-based analysis: reliability, bias, context L4:20% — Extended writing: take a position and defend with evidence

Example HOTS question: "Using sources A, B and C, evaluate the extent to which international pressure ended apartheid. Support your answer with evidence from the sources and your own knowledge. (10 marks)"

English HL & FAL (Languages)

L1 (Literal):10% — Find information directly stated in the text L2 (Reorganisation):15% — Summarise, paraphrase L3 (Inference):30% — Read between the lines, deduce meaning L4 (Evaluation):25% — Critically judge effectiveness, language use L5 (Appreciation):20% — Personal response, emotional/aesthetic engagement

Note: CAPS for Languages uses a 5-level "Barrett's Taxonomy" variant rather than the standard Bloom's 4. Levels 3–5 collectively count as higher-order.

Example HOTS question: "Evaluate the poet's use of contrast in stanzas 2 and 4. To what extent does this contrast strengthen the central theme of loss? Refer closely to the text."

Afrikaans HT & EAT

L1–L2:25% — Letterlike begrip; herorganisering en opsomming L3:30% — Afleiding (lees tussen die reëls) L4:25% — Evaluering van taalgebruik en standpunt L5:20% — Waardering en persoonlike reaksie

Computer Applications Technology (CAT)

L1:30% — Recall hardware/software terms, identify icons L2:25% — Apply skills (apply formatting, write a formula) L3:25% — Combine multiple skills (mail merge with conditional fields) L4:20% — Solve unfamiliar problems, evaluate the best approach

Example HOTS question: "A school wants to track which Grade 12 learners have completed their PAT. Design a spreadsheet solution using at least three different functions, and explain why you chose each."

Information Technology (IT)

L1:20% — Define syntax, recall data types L2:30% — Trace code, predict output L3:30% — Debug a program, modify existing code L4:20% — Design an algorithm or data structure for a new problem

Example HOTS question: "Design and code a Delphi method that, given an array of learner marks, returns both the median and the standard deviation. Justify your choice of data structure."

Engineering Graphics & Design (EGD)

L1:15% — Identify drawing conventions, line types L2:40% — Apply drawing rules to produce orthographic views L3:30% — Convert between 2D and 3D, sectional views L4:15% — Design a component to satisfy given constraints

Tourism

L1:30% — Recall destinations, attractions, terminology L2:30% — Calculate currency conversions, time zones L3:25% — Analyse case studies on tourism trends L4:15% — Plan an itinerary, evaluate sustainable tourism practices

Consumer Studies

L1:25% — Define consumer rights, list nutrients L2:30% — Explain the function of fibre, describe budgeting steps L3:25% — Analyse advertising techniques, compare product labels L4:20% — Recommend a balanced meal for a specific health condition; justify

Visual Arts, Music, Dramatic & Dance Studies

L1:15% — Identify artists, periods, techniques L2:20% — Describe a work or performance L3:30% — Analyse style, technique, influences L4:35% — Critically evaluate; produce / perform an original work

The arts naturally weight HOTS heavily because production and critique are the discipline.

Life Orientation & Religion Studies

L1:25% — Recall human rights articles, religious texts L2:30% — Explain concepts (citizenship, ubuntu) L3:25% — Analyse social issues from multiple perspectives L4:20% — Take a reasoned ethical position on a contemporary dilemma

10. Common Mistakes Teachers Make

1. Confusing the verb with the cognitive demand. "Discuss the causes of WW1" sounds like Analyse, but if learners simply repeat their notes, it's actually Remember.

2. Using "describe" everywhere. "Describe" is Level 2 (Understand). If your whole paper says "describe", you have no Level 3 or 4.

3. Treating multiple-choice as automatically Level 1. A well-designed MCQ can test analysis or evaluation — it depends on the cognitive work needed to choose.

4. Setting a "Create" question with no marks for design quality. If the rubric only rewards correct facts, learners default to Remember.

5. Forgetting the knowledge dimension. All your "Apply" questions might still be procedural recall if you never demand conceptual reasoning.

11. Setter's Quick-Check Checklist

  1. Does my paper hit the CAPS-recommended L1/L2/L3/L4 percentages (within ±5%)?
  2. Have I used different verbs across the paper, not just "explain" and "describe"?
  3. Are my higher-order questions genuinely unfamiliar to the learners (i.e. not seen in the textbook examples)?
  4. Does my marking memorandum allocate marks per cognitive demand, not just per correct fact?
  5. If I used a case study, have I tested all four levels against the same source?
  6. Did I include at least one Level 4 question that asks learners to justify or design?
  7. Have I prepared a cognitive-analysis grid to submit with the paper for moderation?

Final thought: Bloom's Taxonomy is not a checklist — it's a lens. Use it to ask: "What kind of thinking do I want my learners to do today?" When you answer that question honestly, your lessons, your questions and your assessments line up automatically.
